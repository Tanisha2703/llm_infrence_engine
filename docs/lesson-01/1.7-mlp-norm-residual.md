# 1.7 MLP, normalization and residual connections

**Goal:** explain the other half of a decoder layer (everything except attention), show **where the 494M parameters live**,
and justify why the layer is built as "norm, sublayer, add".

## A decoder layer, in two lines
[model.py:149-153](../../src/miniserve/model.py#L149-L153)
```python
x = x + self.self_attn(self.input_layernorm(x), cos, sin)   # communicate between tokens
x = x + self.mlp(self.post_attention_layernorm(x))          # think within each token
```
Attention is *communication* (tokens exchange information). The MLP is *computation* (each token processes what it gathered,
independently of the others). 24 layers repeat the pair.

## The MLP (feed-forward network)
Applied to each token's vector separately: expand 896 → 4,864 numbers, apply a non-linearity, shrink back to 896.
Why expand? A bigger intermediate space gives room to compute many features before compressing.

Qwen uses **SwiGLU**: `down( silu(gate(x)) × up(x) )`, three matrices (see [model.py:137-138](../../src/miniserve/model.py#L137-L138)).
- `up(x)` : the candidate features
- `gate(x)` through `silu` : a per-feature dial between about 0 and 1 saying how much of each candidate to let through
- multiply them, then `down` projects back

`silu(z) = z × sigmoid(z)`, a smooth ReLU-like curve. Gated designs work better than a plain two-matrix MLP at equal size.
Without a non-linearity, stacking layers collapses into one big matrix multiply. The non-linearity is what makes depth useful.

## Where the parameters live (real output, `python examples/lesson1_demos.py 1.7`)
```
per layer: attention 1.84M, MLP 13.07M, norms 1.8K
x24 layers: 358M;  embedding/lm_head (tied, once): 136M;  total 494M
share: MLP 64%, attention 9%, embeddings 28%
```
**Attention is only 9% of the parameters. The MLP is 64%.** (This is why research suggests facts are stored mostly in MLP
layers, which act like key-value memories. Treat that as a hypothesis, not a settled fact.) For serving: speeding up inference
means mostly reading MLP weights fast, which is why quantization (a later stage) targets them.

## RMSNorm
Vectors can drift to large or tiny magnitudes after many layers, making training unstable. Normalization rescales each token's
vector to a standard size.

RMSNorm: `x / rms(x) × weight` with `rms = sqrt(mean(x²))`. Example `x = [3, 4]`:
`rms = sqrt((9+16)/2) = 3.5355`, result `[0.8485, 1.1314]`, whose rms is exactly 1.0. Then multiply by a learned per-feature `weight`.

Versus **LayerNorm**: also subtracts the mean and adds a bias. RMSNorm drops both, so it's cheaper and works about as well.
Code: [model.py:64-68](../../src/miniserve/model.py#L64-L68). Note `x.float()`: we compute in fp32 because summing 896 squares in bf16 (8 bits of
precision) loses accuracy, then cast back.

**Pre-norm vs post-norm:** here the norm sits *before* each sublayer (`x + f(norm(x))`), which trains more stably in deep
stacks than the original post-norm design.

## Residual connections: the `x + ...`
Each sublayer **adds** its output to its input instead of replacing it. Two benefits:
1. **Gradient highway:** during training, gradients flow straight down the `+` path through all 24 layers without vanishing.
2. **Easy to do nothing:** a layer can output ~0 and leave the signal untouched, so extra layers can't make things worse and each
   layer only learns a *refinement*.

Mental model: the **residual stream**, a vector per token flowing from embedding to output. Every attention and MLP block
*reads* from it and *writes* a small update back.

## Interview ladder
**Level 1: What does a transformer layer consist of?**
Pre-norm attention with a residual, then pre-norm MLP with a residual.

**Level 2: Why residual connections?**
They let gradients flow and let layers learn incremental updates; without them very deep networks are barely trainable.

**Level 2: RMSNorm vs LayerNorm?**
RMSNorm only rescales by the root-mean-square (no mean-centering, no bias): fewer operations, similar quality.

**Level 2: What is SwiGLU and why?**
A gated MLP: `silu(gate) × up` then down-projection. Empirically better than ReLU/GELU MLPs for the same parameter budget.

**Level 3: Where are most parameters, and why does it matter for inference?**
In MLPs (64% here) plus embeddings. Decode is memory-bound on reading weights, so compressing these (quantization) directly speeds up decoding.

**Level 3: Why upcast to fp32 inside the norm?**
Reductions over many elements in bf16 accumulate rounding error; fp32 keeps the normalization accurate for negligible cost.

**Level 3: If every layer only adds a small update, what does that say about the 24 layers?**
They progressively refine one shared representation (the residual stream) rather than transform it completely each time.
That's also why techniques such as early exit and layer skipping can work.
